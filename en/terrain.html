4.1 — Who you work with

Who does what, and when to step in

Four roles to identify at the agency — using a Dentsu-style organisation as the example. When you step in matters as much as what you say.

CS
Consulting and strategy

Frames the recommendation and allocates budgets across channels.

→ This is where to step in, before the plan is locked.
DB
Display / multi-channel buyer

Negotiates and buys, through direct deals as well as programmatic.

→ The one who decides to add an Amazon DSP line to the plan.
TD
Trading desk

Traders set up, run and optimise campaigns in the platform.

→ The technical contact, and the first to relay certification.
AO
AdOps and studio

Trafficking, creatives, tags and tracking.

→ Bring them in early: they are the classic bottleneck at go live.
4.2 — The two buying models

Same set-up, two different pitches

You need to know which model you are in before talking price.

Model A

Disclosed programmatic

The agency buys on behalf of the advertiser, who sees the cost breakdown — media CPM, platform fees, data, inventory. The agency’s fee is set by contract.

Our pitch has to hold up in front of the advertiser: measurement, incrementality, inventory quality.
Model B

Non-disclosed programmatic

The agency resells a service at an all-in price, without itemising its buying costs. It carries the risk, and its margin depends on the spread.

The discussion is about: buying cost, guaranteed volumes and deliverability.

Launching a skincare range

Budget of €150,000, over six weeks. Target: urban women aged 25 to 49. Two KPIs chosen: reach and share of new customers.

Week 0 · Brief

Target: urban women aged 25 to 49. Two KPIs chosen: reach and share of new customers. The wrap-up is framed from this point.

Week 0 · Plan
55% CTV, 30% retail display, 15% audio

CTV drives reach, retail display captures intent, audio keeps the thread running. In-market audiences and category buyers.

Week 1 · Setup
One line item per objective

Frequency cap of 3 exposures per week across screens. Programmatic Guaranteed on key moments, open auction and Private Auction for the rest.

Week 2 · In flight
12% of the display budget moves to CTV

CTV shows a better cost per new customer. Low-viewability placements are excluded at the same time.

What is negotiable

  • Platform fees, based on volume.
  • The cost of data, when relying on first-party audiences.
  • Programmatic Guaranteed commitments.

What isn’t

The media CPM in the open auction, which is set for every impression by competition. No negotiation applies there — only managing the maximum CPM and the supply path has an effect.

The right question to ask: “What share of the budget ends up as served impressions?” A low CPM with a long supply chain often costs more than a higher CPM bought direct.

Indicative ranges: the actual split depends on the contract, the channel mix and the share of third-party data.

4.5 — The five common objections

The objection on the front, the structured answer on the back. Everyone should be able to run through them without hesitating.

Objection 01

“It’s a walled garden, I won’t see anything.”

Flip →
Answer

Performance is visible: reporting by placement, measurement all the way to the sale, cross-dataset analysis in a clean room. What stays closed is individual-level data — not the results.

Objection 02

“I don’t sell on Amazon.”

Flip →
Answer

Amazon DSP is open to non-endemic advertisers. The signals are used for targeting; the conversion can happen on your site, at a dealership or in store.

Objection 03

“I already have DV360, why add another DSP?”

Flip →
Answer

It’s not a replacement, it’s a complement: Prime Video, Twitch, Fire TV and shopping signals aren’t available anywhere else. We prove it on a small share of the budget.

Objection 04

“Your CPMs are more expensive.”

Flip →
Answer

Compare like with like: premium CTV in a logged-in environment can’t be compared with open-web display. The real arbiter is cost per outcome.

Objection 05

“Too complex, I don’t have the resources.”

Flip →
Answer

Managed or self-service depending on the team’s maturity. We start on a narrow scope, with support and training for the traders.

The general rule: never defend a CPM. Bring it back to cost per outcome, then propose a structured test — scope, duration, success criterion.
4.6 — The five setup mistakes

Ten minutes of checks, half of all bad wrap-ups avoided

The mistakeNo frequency cap, or one set too high
What it costs

The budget concentrates on heavy media consumers and reach stalls.

The reflex

Set the cap at the brief, across screens, and review it in week 2.

The mistakeA single line item for several objectives
What it costs

Optimisation targets the wrong signal and the wrap-up becomes unreadable.

The reflex

One objective, one line item, one KPI. Always.

The mistakeTracking added after launch
What it costs

The first days are lost for measurement and the wrap-up becomes a negotiation.

The reflex

Validate pixels and tags before go live, not after.

The mistakeAudiences too narrow at launch
What it costs

Too little volume for optimisation to learn, and the CPM shoots up.

The reflex

Start broad, then narrow down to what performs.

The mistakeForgotten exclusions
What it costs

You pay again for customers you already have and run ads where you didn’t want them.

The reflex

Exclude existing customers and apply the lists from setup.

Chapter 05

Key takeaways

Unified buying, first-party signals, consolidated measurement.

Session summary
Four sentences you should be able to repeat a week from now.
  • Buying happens impression by impression, in around a hundred milliseconds — and mostly in first price, which makes the bid a direct cost lever.
  • A campaign is built in order: objective, data, inventory, setup, optimisation, measurement. One primary objective, one line item per objective.
  • Addressability no longer relies on third-party cookies: authenticated signals, context, first-party IDs and publisher data work together.
  • Audio, CTV and DOOH are going programmatic, and are bought from a single entry point.
