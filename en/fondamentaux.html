The fundamentals

1.1
1.1 — Definition

What “programmatic” means

Programmatic is the automated buying and selling of advertising space, one impression at a time, through technology platforms.

No more insertion orders negotiated and exchanged by hand: each impression is assessed individually based on its context, the audience seeing it and the campaign objective. Every publisher becomes reachable from a single entry point.

The way to put it to a client: you no longer buy a placement for a week, you buy an opportunity to reach someone, one at a time, making a call every single time.

Speed

The impression is auctioned, bought and served in under 200 milliseconds, while the page is loading.

Scope

Display, video, audio, CTV and DOOH are all bought from the same platform, across five screens.

Unified data

The same profile is recognised across every publisher, and can be enriched with third-party data.

Global frequency capping

Repeat exposure is managed globally, not publisher by publisher.

1.2 — The ecosystem

Four players and two cross-cutting layers

Read the chain from demand to supply. The DSP connects directly to publishers’ SSPs, and the whole transaction happens while the page is loading.

Signals and data

First-party audiences, third-party data as an extra layer, page context, device type, geography: everything that feeds the bidding decision.

Ad server and measurement

Creative delivery, impression counting, brand safety checks and consolidated measurement, continuously.

The reflex to keep: DSP = buy side, SSP = sell side. It is the most common mix-up in meetings — and the easiest one to clear up.

What happens at step 3

Filtering. An audience that is too narrow, a frequency cap already reached, a badly set brand safety list — and the campaign doesn’t even compete.

What happens at step 4

Valuation. The maximum CPM entered in the line item becomes, in first price, the price actually paid.

What happens at step 7

Measurement. Without tags and pixels validated before go live, the impression is served but unreadable at wrap-up.

1.4 — Pricing models

First price and second price

Two auction logics have followed one another in the market. Move the slider: the price paid is recalculated in both models, against the same competitors.

Pricing model comparator
Competing bids received by the SSP

Second price former standard

The winner paid the second-highest bid plus one cent.

—

You could bid high without paying high: the gap between the bid entered and the price actually paid absorbed valuation errors.

First price today’s standard

The winner pays exactly its maximum bid.

—

Every cent bid is a cent spent. The bid becomes a cost lever, no longer just a statement of intent.

Operational consequence: manage the maximum CPM by audience and by placement, and keep a constant eye on drift in the actual cost. A maximum CPM set once at setup and then forgotten is a budget line that leaks.

Media based or audience based

Media based — buying a placement

Space on a site, in a section or a programme whose audience is assumed. It is the logic of traditional media planning.

  • Premium, controlled context, high-impact formats
  • Relies on publisher data, without depending on an identifier
  • Audience assumed, not measured individually
  • Frequency capping hard to maintain from one publisher to another

Audience based — buying people

People qualified by data, whatever the site where you find them. It is the logic of audience planning.

  • Individual precision, cross-publisher capping and deduplication
  • Volume available immediately, real-time optimisation
  • Depends on signal availability
  • Inventory sometimes less premium
In practice, the two are combined: an audience-based foundation for precision, topped up with media-based buys on high-value contexts.

1.8 — The four deal types

From the most open to the most reserved

Each deal takes the form of a Deal ID activated in the DSP. Priority access to the impression follows the order below.

AccessReserved, negotiated directly
PriceFixed CPM
VolumeGuaranteed and reserved

Typical use: key moments, sponsorship, scarce inventory such as premium CTV. It is the only deal that reserves impressions — the one you pull out when reach must be secured on a given date.

Typical use: priority on inventory, with no volume commitment. Not to be confused with PG: you jump the queue, you don’t reserve anything.

Typical use: premium inventory, competition limited to a few buyers. The right balance between inventory quality and bidding flexibility.

Typical use: broad prospecting, scale at a controlled cost. This is where managing the maximum CPM and exclusions makes the biggest difference.

1.9 — Inventory access priority

Priority beats price

An impression doesn’t go to the highest bidder: it goes first to the highest-ranked deal in the ad server. Switch the tiers off one by one to see who serves it.

Ad server decision · click a tier to disable it
1 Programmatic Guaranteed Reserved volume, CPM negotiated in advance €12.00 fixed CPM
2 Preferred Deal Priority access, fixed CPM, no volume commitment €9.50 fixed CPM
3 Private Auction Invitation-only auction, with a floor price €14.20 best bid
4 Open Auction Auction open to all buyers €16.40 best bid
A nuance to know: this ladder describes the ad server’s delivery priority. Between the two auction tiers (Private and Open), the unified auctions of modern ad servers now largely decide on price. What holds true everywhere: a reserved or negotiated agreement comes before an auction, whatever the amount.
1.9 — On the publisher side

Waterfall or header bidding

Before it even reaches the ad server, the publisher has to call its SSPs. There are two ways to do it — and the difference shows up in both time and price.

SSP calls · waterfall versus header bidding
Publisher floor price — The minimum a bid must reach to be accepted. It is the same in both methods, with exactly the same bids.
Waterfall in sequence

SSPs are called one after another. As soon as a bid reaches the floor, it wins the impression and the process stops: the rest are never called.

Winning CPM—
Header bidding in parallel

A wrapper in the page (most often Prebid.js) calls every SSP at the same time. Bids below the floor are eliminated, then the best of the eligible bids is selected.

Total latency—
Winning CPM—

What it changes for us, on the buy side

  • The same inventory can arrive through several SSPs at once: that is exactly the duplication Supply Path Optimisation tracks down.
  • The winning header bidding bid is only a price to beat passed to the ad server — it then goes up against direct lines according to the priority seen above.
  • A Deal ID is an identified path: it goes through header bidding without being diluted in open competition.

The line to remember

Header bidding put an end to “first come, first served”. Priority hasn’t moved.

A publisher that moves to header bidding raises its revenue per impression, but doesn’t give up serving its reserved agreements first. The two mechanisms stack on top of each other; they don’t replace one another.

The question to ask in a meeting

“What share of the budget ends up as served impressions?”

A low CPM with a long supply chain often costs more than a higher CPM bought direct. It is the argument that shifts the conversation from price to value.

These orders of magnitude come from market studies. Reassess them campaign by campaign using the DSP’s reports.

Key takeaways — chapter 1
The right inventory, the right audience, at the right price — automatically.
  • Buying happens impression by impression, in around a hundred milliseconds.
  • Compared with siloed direct deals, programmatic unifies data, frequency capping and measurement across publishers.
  • From Open Auction to Programmatic Guaranteed, the deal is chosen based on the objective and how scarce the inventory is.
  • The bid, the supply path and the choice of DSP are three cost levers you can manage.
